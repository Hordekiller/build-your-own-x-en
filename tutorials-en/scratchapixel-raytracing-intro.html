Introduction to Ray Tracing: a Simple Method for Creating 3D Images

About

This Scratchapixel lesson explains, from first principles, why ray tracing is the simplest model of image synthesis. It builds the idea from real-world physics — light travelling along straight lines — and shows how simulating those light paths backwards (from camera to light source) produces photorealistic images without the approximations of the rasterization pipeline.

What you'll learn

  • Why rays of light can be treated as straight lines (geometric optics).
  • The conceptual difference between rasterization and ray tracing.
  • How a camera model — a position and an image plane — turns into rays through pixels.
  • How intersection tests, shadows, reflections and refractions are all the same operation.
  • The math vocabulary of a raytracer: rays, directions, t-parameters and normals.

Core idea

A raytracer inverts reality: instead of waiting for light to hit the eye, it shoots a ray from the eye through every pixel of the image and asks "what did this ray hit first?". The color of that hit — modulated by light sources, shadows and material — becomes the pixel's color.

C++// The heart of every raytracer: cast one ray per pixel and find its color
for (int y = 0; y < imageHeight; ++y) {
  for (int x = 0; x < imageWidth; ++x) {
    Ray ray = shootRay(x, y);         // ray from camera through pixel
    Color c = castRay(ray);           // first hit of the ray in the scene
    framebuffer[y * imageWidth + x] = c;
  }
}

Why it matters

Ray tracing is the rendering algorithm behind movie VFX and modern game engines — and its core fits in a few dozen lines. This lesson gives you the mental model before ever touching code, which makes every later raytracer (including "Ray Tracing in One Weekend") dramatically easier to follow.
